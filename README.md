**Use this when** you have to send something written and you want it to be a
decision, not a brochure they forward around for comparison.

## What I do

I write four things and refuse to write a fifth: the outcome, the number, what
is not included, and what happens when they say yes. Capability lists, team
bios, and methodology diagrams do not go in, because none of them has ever
been the reason somebody signed.

Every figure in it comes from you. I will not price your work.

## The four parts

**1. The outcome, in their words.** One sentence about their situation after
this is done, using the language they used on the call. Not what you deliver,
what changes. If you cannot write this sentence, you do not understand the deal
well enough to price it.

**2. The number.** One number, visible, near the top. If there are tiers, say
which one you recommend and why, in a sentence, or they will compare the tiers
to each other instead of comparing you to doing nothing.

**3. What is not included.** This is the section that closes. It prevents the
scope fight, it tells them you have done this before, and it makes the included
list believable by contrast. Name the three or four things a reasonable person
would assume are in and are not.

**4. What happens on yes.** The literal next physical action. Who signs what,
when the first thing happens, what you need from them in week one. A proposal
with no instruction ends in "looks great, we will discuss internally."

## Two rules on the writing

- Nothing in the deliverables that you did not commit to out loud. A proposal
  is the document you will be held to, and an invented timeline or an extra
  deliverable added to pad the page is a promise you now owe.
- No number anywhere that you did not give me. Not a projected saving, not a
  payback period, not a percentage. If a number would help the case, the
  proposal names the thing they should measure instead.

## Limits

I cannot price your work and will not try, so a proposal missing its number
stays missing it. I do not know what your contract requires, what your lawyer
would add, or what their procurement process demands, and in a large company
procurement overrides a good proposal entirely.

The trap: the brochure. The naive version produces eight pages of capability,
buries one price on page six, and includes no exclusions, so it reads as a menu
and gets stacked against two other menus. The second trap is quieter: padding
the deliverables to make the price look fair. Everything you add to justify a
number is work you have just agreed to do for free.

## How this one knows what it knows

Every answer it gives itself carries a label: `CHECKED` (it verified it,
and says how), `FROM YOU` (you said it, and it quotes where), or
`GUESSED` (it could not verify). Then it counts the guesses, so you
correct what is wrong instead of trusting confident output built on
silent assumptions.

> Never ask for something you can check.
> Never assume something you cannot check.
